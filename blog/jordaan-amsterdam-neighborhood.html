The Jordaan: Amsterdam's Most Charming Neighborhood

WanderAmsterdam editorial · Published 2026-10-22

The Jordaan is the neighborhood that visitors most often describe as the "real Amsterdam" — a dense web of narrow canals, 17th-century houses, independent galleries, antique shops, food markets, and brown cafés wedged into the western edge of the UNESCO canal ring. It is compact enough to walk across in 20 minutes and rich enough to spend a full day in without exhausting.

The neighborhood was laid out in 1613 as part of Amsterdam's great expansion, built as a working-class quarter for tanners, brewers, and weavers — and for the political and religious refugees who flooded into the city during the Dutch Golden Age. Many of its residents were Huguenots, Protestant immigrants who fled France to escape persecution. They called the district "le jardin" — the garden — because its streets were named after flowers and plants. The Dutch corruption of that became "Jordaan."

What the Jordaan looks like

Unlike the grand canal ring beside it, the Jordaan was built for working people. Its canal houses are smaller, its streets narrower, its alleys (steegjes) more numerous. The result is an intimate scale that feels different from the rest of Amsterdam's center. Most buildings are four or five storeys of red brick, with the characteristic Dutch step gables and the hook and beam at the top for hauling furniture through the windows — houses here are still too narrow for staircases wide enough to move large objects.

Hofjes: The Jordaan contains a series of hofjes — hidden courtyard almshouses built between the 17th and 19th centuries as charitable housing for elderly women. Many are accessible during the day through narrow doorways in ordinary-looking street facades. The Begijnhof (just outside the Jordaan proper) and the Hofje van Brienen are among the most atmospheric.

Where to spend time

Noordermarkt

The square around the Noorderkerk hosts two of Amsterdam's most interesting markets: a farmers' market on Saturday mornings (organic produce, bread, and cheese from Dutch farms) and a flea and antiques market on Monday mornings. The Noorderkerk itself is a Protestant church from 1623, built as a square with a central plan — one of the earliest examples of a Reformed church layout in the Netherlands.

The Nine Streets (De Negen Straatjes)

Nine short cross-streets connecting the Jordaan's parallel canals have become a destination for independent shopping — vintage clothing, specialty food, design objects, and bookshops. The streets are at their best on a quiet weekday, before the Saturday crowds arrive.

Galleries and studios

The Jordaan has a long association with Amsterdam's art community. Dozens of small galleries line the canals and side streets, showing contemporary Dutch and international work. Most are open Wednesday through Saturday; browsing is casual and pressure-free.

Exploring on foot or by walking tour

The Jordaan rewards slow walking more than almost any Amsterdam neighborhood. Guided Amsterdam walking tours through the Jordaan cover the history of the neighborhood, its notable residents (including Rembrandt, who lived on Rozengracht from 1656 to 1669), and its hidden courtyards in roughly two hours.
