Amsterdam's Bike Culture: Why the City Has More Bikes Than People

WanderAmsterdam editorial · Published 2026-10-22

Amsterdam has an estimated 900,000 bicycles for a city of roughly 940,000 people. That means more bikes than residents — a ratio that has no equivalent in a major Western city. Bicycles account for around 36 percent of all traffic movements in Amsterdam. Nearly 70 percent of commuters and students get to work or school by bike. Over 400 kilometers of dedicated cycle lanes thread through the city. The bicycle is not a leisure activity or a sustainable transport option here: it is simply how Amsterdam moves.

How Amsterdam became a cycling city

Amsterdam was actually a car-dominated city in the 1960s and early 1970s, with roads widened for traffic and canals filled in to create parking space. The oil crisis of 1973 — combined with growing protests against car-caused child casualties — triggered a political shift. The city began investing in cycling infrastructure in the late 1970s, a commitment that has been maintained and expanded ever since.

The result is a cycling network so comprehensive that it is genuinely faster to cross most of Amsterdam by bicycle than by car or tram. A bike lane runs alongside almost every major road. Intersections have separate signal phases for cyclists. Bicycle parking facilities accommodate tens of thousands of bikes at Amsterdam Centraal station alone.

The Amsterdam bike aesthetic: Visitors often expect sporty, light bicycles. Amsterdam bikes are typically the opposite: heavy, upright, single or three-speed, with a back-pedal brake, fenders, a chain guard, built-in lights, and a rear rack for cargo. These "omafiets" (grandmother bikes) are designed for comfort and durability on flat, short urban trips — not speed. You will look odd on a racing bike in the Jordaan.

The rules — written and unwritten

Amsterdam's cycling culture has its own informal code. Cyclists ride on the right, use hand signals for turns, and move smoothly and predictably. Pedestrians who wander into cycle lanes are a genuine hazard — the lanes are not tourist attractions but working infrastructure, and Amsterdam cyclists will ring their bells directly and persistently at anyone blocking the flow.

  • Always use the cycle lane (fietspad), never the road or pavement
  • Lock your bike to a fixed point — unlocked bikes are stolen within minutes in central areas
  • Do not use your phone while cycling; this is both dangerous and illegal in the Netherlands
  • Lights are legally required after dark
  • Right of way works the same as driving; yield to the right at unmarked intersections

Cycling as a visitor

Renting a bike for a day or two is one of the best ways to see Amsterdam. The flat terrain makes cycling accessible to most fitness levels, and even a few hours on a bike gives a completely different experience of the city from walking or public transport. The key is confidence: hesitant cyclists struggling in busy lanes create more danger than decisive ones who commit to their direction.

Guided bike tours are available for visitors who prefer an introduction to the cycling culture and infrastructure before heading out solo. Amsterdam bike tour operators run routes through the Jordaan, along the canal ring, into the Haarlem area, and through Amsterdam Noord — typically two to three hours, at a pace suitable for casual cyclists.

The cargo bike revolution

Amsterdam is currently a global reference point for cargo cycling. The bakfiets (box bike) — a low-slung cargo bicycle with a wooden box over the front wheel — has replaced the car for family transport in significant numbers of Amsterdam households. On any given morning near school drop-off time, the city's cycle lanes carry dozens of these vehicles loaded with children, groceries, or construction materials. The cargo bike is now an emblem of Amsterdam family life as distinctive as the canal house or the brown café.
