The Rijksmuseum & Rembrandt's Night Watch: Complete Visitor Guide

WanderAmsterdam editorial · Published 2026-10-22

The Rijksmuseum is the Netherlands' national museum and one of the most visited museums in the world. Its permanent collection spans Dutch and Flemish art and history from the Middle Ages to the 20th century — over a million objects, with around 8,000 on display at any time. At its center, in a purpose-built gallery called the Gallery of Honour, hangs the painting that draws visitors from every continent: Rembrandt van Rijn's The Night Watch.

The Night Watch: what you're actually looking at

Completed in 1642, The Night Watch measures 363 by 437 centimeters — roughly the size of a large garage door. It depicts a militia company under the command of Captain Frans Banninck Cocq, painted at the moment of marching out. What made it revolutionary was not the subject but the treatment: rather than the stiff, posed group portraits typical of the era, Rembrandt captured motion, drama, and three-dimensional light.

The painting's popular name is misleading. Despite its dark reputation, it is almost certainly a daytime scene — centuries of varnish and grime darkened the canvas, and 18th-century viewers assumed it showed night. Cleaning and research have confirmed the painting depicts a moment of bright, directional sunlight.

Size surprise: The Night Watch was actually larger when first painted. In 1715, when the painting was moved to Amsterdam's Town Hall, it was trimmed on all four sides to fit through a doorway. The original left edge — showing two additional figures — is known only from a small 17th-century copy in the Rijksmuseum's own collection.

Beyond the Night Watch: what else to see

The Gallery of Honour leading to the Night Watch is lined with other major Rembrandt paintings and works by Frans Hals, Jan Steen, and Jacob van Ruisdael. But the Rijksmuseum collection extends far beyond Dutch Golden Age painting:

  • Vermeer room: Four paintings by Johannes Vermeer, including The Milkmaid and Woman Reading a Letter — small, luminous canvases that reward slow looking.
  • Delft Blue: A major collection of Delftware pottery, the blue-and-white ceramics that became one of the Netherlands' most recognizable exports.
  • Ships and navigation: Scale models of Dutch East India Company vessels, navigation instruments, and maps from the age of exploration.
  • Asian Art pavilion: Objects collected during Dutch trading in Japan, China, and Southeast Asia — including Edo-period Japanese art and Ming dynasty ceramics.
  • Garden: The museum's outdoor garden contains sculpture, canal boats, and historic gatehouse fragments. Entry is free.

Practical visitor information

Tickets and timing

Timed-entry tickets are required and must be booked in advance at rijksmuseum.nl. Tickets cost around €22.50 for adults (subject to change). The museum opens at 9:00 AM daily. The quietest times are before 10:30 AM and after 4:00 PM. Wednesday through Friday mornings are typically less crowded than weekends.

How long to allow

A focused visit covering the highlights takes around 90 minutes. A thorough visit takes three hours or more. The building itself — an 1885 neo-Gothic and Dutch Renaissance design by Pierre Cuypers, restored and reopened in 2013 after a decade-long renovation — is worth exploring as an architectural object.

Museumplein

The Rijksmuseum sits on Museumplein, a large public square shared with the Van Gogh Museum and the Stedelijk Museum of modern art. Visiting all three in a single day is possible but demanding; most visitors choose one or two.

Getting there

Tram lines 2, 11, and 12 stop directly at Museumplein. The Rijksmuseum has a dedicated cycle parking facility — fitting for Amsterdam. Museum-focused walking tours and guided museum visits are available through Amsterdam museum tour operators who provide context and priority access.
