12 Best Museums in Amsterdam

WanderAmsterdam editorial · Published 2026-08-30

Amsterdam has more museums per square kilometer than any city in the world. Over 70 museums in a city of 900,000 people — covering everything from Rembrandt to resistance, tulips to torture, microbes to modern art. The big three (Rijksmuseum, Van Gogh, Anne Frank House) are but some of the smaller museums are just as memorable.

Here are the 12 worth your time, with tips on booking, timing, and what to see first.

1. Rijksmuseum

The national museum — and the best in the Netherlands

Eight thousand objects across 80 galleries covering 800 years of Dutch art and history. The Gallery of Honour builds toward Rembrandt's Night Watch, recently restored and displayed with its missing sections reconstructed using AI. Vermeer's Milkmaid, Frans Hals portraits, Delft pottery, and the Cuypers Library (the most beautiful room in the building) are all here. Even the gardens are free to walk.

~€22.50 · Allow 2–3 hours
Tip: Book the 9am slot — the Gallery of Honour is nearly empty for the first 30 minutes. The museum shop and café are excellent.

2. Van Gogh Museum

200 paintings and 500 drawings — the world's largest Van Gogh collection

The chronological layout follows Van Gogh from his dark Dutch period (The Potato Eaters) through Paris, Arles, Saint-Rémy, and Auvers-sur-Oise. Sunflowers, The Bedroom, Almond Blossom, and Wheatfield with Crows are all here, along with letters to his brother Theo that add context you won't get from the paintings alone. The audio guide is worth the extra cost.

~€20 · Allow 1.5–2 hours

3. Anne Frank House

The Secret Annex on Prinsengracht

The house where Anne Frank and seven others hid for two years during the Nazi occupation. Walking through the bookcase entrance into the annex — seeing Anne's magazine clippings still on the walls, the pencil marks tracking the children's heights — is devastating and essential. The museum has been carefully updated without losing its power. Book well ahead.

~€16 · Book 6 weeks ahead
Tip: Tickets release 6 weeks before the visit date at 10am CET. Set a reminder — they sell out within hours. A limited number of evening slots are sometimes available closer to the date.

4. Stedelijk Museum

Modern and contemporary art on Museumplein

The third Museumplein museum gets less foot traffic than its neighbors, which is a shame — the Stedelijk's collection of modern and contemporary art rivals MoMA. Mondrian, Malevich, Karel Appel, Yayoi Kusama, Willem de Kooning, and strong design and photography sections. The permanent collection alone justifies the ticket; the temporary exhibitions often steal the show.

~€22.50 · Allow 1.5 hours

5. FOAM Photography Museum

World-class photography in a canal house

FOAM occupies a canal house on Keizersgracht and rotates exhibitions roughly every three months. The curation is consistently sharp — recent shows have covered Viviane Sassen, Anton Corbijn, and Daido Moriyama. The intimate rooms force you to engage with each image. It's small enough to see in an hour and always worth checking what's on before your visit.

~€14 · Allow 1 hour

6. Rembrandt House Museum

Where Rembrandt lived and painted for 20 years

Rembrandt bought this house on Jodenbreestraat in 1639 at the height of his success (and eventually lost it to bankruptcy). The rooms are restored to their 17th-century appearance using the inventory from his insolvency filing — so we know exactly what was in each room. Daily etching demonstrations show his printmaking techniques. A more intimate experience than the Rijksmuseum.

~€17.50 · Allow 1 hour

7. NEMO Science Museum

Hands-on science — the best museum for kids

Five floors of interactive experiments covering physics, technology, biology, and energy. Designed for children but engaging enough that adults enjoy it too — the bubble room, the chain-reaction machines, and the finance exhibit are highlights. The rooftop terrace (free to access, no ticket needed) has one of the best panoramic views in Amsterdam — the Oosterdok, Centraal Station, and the IJ waterway.

~€18.50 · Allow 2–3 hours

8. Dutch Resistance Museum (Verzetsmuseum)

How ordinary Dutch people responded to Nazi occupation

This museum tells the story of the German occupation through the choices Dutch citizens faced — collaboration, resistance, or something in between. The objects are personal: forged identity cards, hidden radios, illegal newspapers. The children's exhibition (Verzetsmuseum Junior) tells the same period through the experiences of four Dutch children. One of the most thoughtful WWII museums anywhere.

~€14 · Allow 1.5 hours

9. EYE Film Museum

Film museum in a striking building across the IJ

The EYE sits on the north bank of the IJ — take the free ferry from Centraal Station (5 minutes). The building by Delugan Meissl is worth seeing from outside alone. Inside: rotating film exhibitions, four screening rooms showing everything from silent films to avant-garde, and a café-restaurant with waterfront views. The lobby installations are free; exhibitions require a ticket.

~€12 · Free ferry

10. Moco Museum

Banksy, Kusama, and contemporary art on Museumplein

A privately owned museum in a townhouse on Museumplein featuring works by Banksy, Kusama, KAWS, Haring, and Basquiat alongside digital art installations. The immersive rooms — particularly the Digital Immersive Art wing — are designed for the Instagram age, but the Banksy collection is substantial and the curation is better than "selfie museum." Divisive among art purists, popular with everyone else.

~€22.50 · Allow 1–1.5 hours

11. Micropia

The world's only microbe museum

Next to Artis Zoo, Micropia displays living microorganisms under microscopes — bacteria, viruses, fungi, and algae made visible and explained. You scan your own body for microbes at the entrance. The Kiss-o-Meter measures bacteria transferred in a kiss. It sounds niche but the execution is fascinating, especially for older kids and science-minded adults. There's nothing else like it.

~€18 · Allow 1 hour

12. Museum Van Loon

A canal house as it was meant to be lived in

One of the few canal houses open to the public with its original furnishings intact. The Van Loon family has lived here since 1884; the house itself dates to 1672. Period rooms, family portraits, a formal garden, and a coach house give you a sense of what life was like inside these famous facades. It's small, personal, and the opposite of a blockbuster museum — which is exactly the point.

~€15 · Allow 45 min
